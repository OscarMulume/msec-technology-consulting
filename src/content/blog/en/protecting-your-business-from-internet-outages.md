An internet outage should not stop a sale, a payment or an order. Yet many businesses depend on a single network link and on software that refuses to start without a connection. The right answer is not to promise that the connection will never fail: it is to prepare the business to keep operating when it does.

## 1. Identify what must keep running

Start with the operations that cannot wait: taking payments, printing or sending a receipt, checking a price, recording an order, contacting a supplier. For each operation, note the acceptable delay and what happens today during an outage.

This list prevents investment in equipment that does not meet the real need. It also helps set priorities: the point of sale and stock often come before internal messaging.

## 2. Choose local-first operation

Useful point-of-sale software must be able to record a sale on the device, even without a remote server. Synchronisation with the server comes afterwards, when the connection returns. In this architecture:

- the sale is recorded locally with a unique identifier;
- payment methods are recorded at the time of payment;
- synchronisation picks up pending operations without creating duplicates;
- a screen or log shows what has not yet been synchronised.

Always ask the supplier how conflicts, duplicates and offline operations are handled. A vague answer is a risk.

## 3. Plan a second network path

A single connection remains a single point of failure. Depending on the site, a combination of fibre, 4G and satellite can reduce dependence on one operator. The failover must be tested: disconnect the main link, check that the point of sale and critical workstations regain connectivity, then document the procedure.

Redundancy only has value when it is verified. Untested backup equipment is a hope, not a plan.

## 4. Write the outage procedure

A short procedure, available offline, should state:

- who informs the team and how;
- which operations remain possible offline;
- how to record payments and orders that are not synchronised;
- who checks that synchronisation resumes;
- who contacts support and with what information.

This procedure should fit on one page and be known by the people who open and close the business.

## 5. Back up the data that matters

Sales, stock, customers and accounting files must exist somewhere other than on a single computer. Keep several copies, on different media, including one off site. Check regularly that restoration is possible: an untested backup does not protect the business.

## 6. Review after every incident

After an outage, compare local and synchronised sales, check payments and note what went wrong. A documented incident becomes a concrete improvement for next time.

## What to remember

Continuity does not rely on a promise of total availability. It relies on local operations, a second network path, verified backups and a known procedure. This is progressive work, which can start with the most critical operations in the business.
